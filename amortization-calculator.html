How an Amortization Schedule Works

A fixed-rate loan has one payment for its whole life, calculated as M = P × r(1+r)n ÷ [(1+r)n − 1], where P is the loan, r the monthly rate and n the number of payments. Each month, interest = remaining balance × r. The rest of the payment reduces principal.

Example: $200,000 at 7% for 30 years

  • Monthly payment: $1,330.60
  • Month 1 interest: $1,166.67 — only $163.93 reduces the balance
  • Total interest over 30 years: about $279,000

Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is at its highest. The split flips over time: in the final years almost the whole payment is principal.

💡 Adding $100 a month to that $200,000 loan pays it off about 5 years 9 months early and saves roughly $63,000 in interest. Enter it in the Extra Monthly Payment box to see your own numbers.

Before you prepay

  • Confirm the loan has no prepayment penalty.
  • Tell the lender to apply extra money to principal, not to next month's payment.
  • Keep an emergency fund first — money paid into a mortgage is hard to get back out.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a biweekly payment plan worth it? ▼
Paying half your monthly payment every two weeks makes 26 half-payments — 13 full payments a year instead of 12. The effect is the same as adding one-twelfth of a payment each month. Avoid services that charge a fee for this; you can do it yourself.
Why do early payments go to interest? ▼
Interest is calculated on remaining balance. Early on, balance is highest so interest is highest. More of each payment becomes principal over time.
How much do extra payments save? ▼
$100-200 extra/month on a typical mortgage saves tens of thousands and cuts years off the loan.
What is negative amortization? ▼
When minimum payment doesn't cover interest, balance grows instead of shrinking. Avoid loans with this feature.
